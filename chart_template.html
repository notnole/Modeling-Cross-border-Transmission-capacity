JAO monthly capacity rights · two models, one order

Bid the lower of two fair values, and only where both of them clear

Two models price the same transmission right from disjoint inputs. One is a Margrabe spread option on the EEX forward curve with a volatility fitted to day-ahead history — no fundamentals. The other is the capacities project’s LGBM day-ahead forecast off 64 Volue fundamentals curves — no forwards. This page is about the rule that submits one order at the lower of the two.

bid = min( margrabe_value , fundamental_bid )

JAO monthly auctions are marginal-priced, so the bid level does nothing except decide whether you win — you pay the clearing price either way. That makes this rule exactly equivalent to “buy only where both models independently clear the price”, because min(a,b) ≥ c if and only if a ≥ c and b ≥ c. Verified on the data: identical win set, identical P&L to the cent. It is also why the rule beats the average and the max of the same two numbers, which is the first thing below.

Every auction is priced as of its own bid gate closure, a median 12 days before delivery starts, from information that predates the gate.

Read this first

P&L is EUR at 1 MW on each corridor: (realised value − clearing price) × delivery hours. Realised value is mean(max(P_to − P_from, 0)) over the delivered hours — a non-nominated right is paid the coupling differential under UIOSI, so a negative spread pays 0, not a loss.

One thing is deliberately not modelled: we assume a price taker — our bid never moves the clearing price. Plausible at 1 MW against 30-plus-bidder books, but unverified.

Maintenance is not a discount. DE(50Hz)-CZ averages 172 h a month of reduced ATC with maintenance_min_atc_mw of 0, so even 1 MW is physically out — but under HAR Art 59 a curtailed holder is made whole at the same max(spread, 0), and all 7,728 observed curtailment records read Network Security, not force majeure. The residual is the Art 59(2)–(4) compensation cap, which cuts holders pro rata and is not quantifiable without JAO’s published cap figures.

Both models are scored against this project’s realised values so the target is identical. They agree to 0.016 EUR/MWh on average anyway, computing the same payoff from independent price pulls.

Why the lower, and not the average or the max

Marginal pricing means the bid level only selects the win set, so the three combinations are nested: everything the lower bid buys, the average buys, and everything the average buys, the max buys. Each relaxation is therefore testable on the auctions it adds — the last two rows price exactly those.

The mechanism: disagreement is the signal to stay out

Mean P&L per auction in each cell of the 2×2. The agreement bid is the top-left cell alone. It is not that agreement is magic — it is that whenever one model buys and the other declines the average auction loses money, and the lower bid is the only one of the three combinations that excludes every disagreement.

Month by month

Bars are summed across the corridors offered that month, so a month where the rule buys nothing sits at zero rather than going missing. Perfect foresight buys only the rights that turned out profitable — a bound, not a rival strategy.

Cumulative, and the honest control

The dashed line is the agreement bid with both rich border groups removed — DE-AT/AT-DE and the four German-Czech corridors, where the auction left money on the table regardless of any model. That is the control that matters: on the corridors where nothing was being given away, does the rule still add anything?

Per corridor

The one split that changes how to read all of this

The fundamental bid is p50 × k, where k is a median of realised / p50 over that corridor’s own completed months. Early in the window there is not enough history, so k defaults to exactly 1.0 and the bid is the raw p50 — which runs about +1.8 EUR/MWh high and therefore can barely act as a floor. That splits the window in two, and the rule is a different animal in each.

Why neither model alone

Rule-independent: each estimate against what the right turned out to be worth. Shorter is better. Watch the bias column for the lower-of-two — taking a minimum is deliberately conservative, so it understates value by construction. That is a feature for a bidder, and it is why the rule is not the most accurate row here.
